FIELD GUIDE / STEWARDSHIP RUNTIME

Grafting field guide

Biology, methods, timing and stewardship explained without treating every branch as an experiment.

What grafting actually does

Rootstock

The living roots and supporting structure of the host tree. Influences vigor, resilience and mature size, but only among compatible tissue.

Scion

Bud or twig from a known fruiting cultivar. It produces that cultivar's leaves, flowers and fruit when successfully established.

The cambium must connect

Living cambial layers immediately beneath bark must align, remain moist and form functional vascular connections. A green bud in year one is not proof of a lasting union.

Method selection · Practical overview

A

Whip & tongue

Bench grafting matching slender diameters while stock is dormant. Effective with skill and secure wrapping.

B

Cleft graft

Topworking some compatible existing trees. An arborist should assess wound size, height, wood condition and branch mechanics.

C

Budding

Join an individual bud to compatible stock when seasonal bark/tissue conditions are appropriate; common in citrus nursery propagation.

Six-phase professional workflow

  1. Identify & photograph. Botanical species, cultivar if known, leaf/bud/fruit morphology, health and rootstock history.
  2. Confirm permission. Private ownership or city arborist/public-tree authority; obtain written consent before cuts.
  3. Screen ecology. Invasiveness, cross-border scion restrictions, pests and fungi, frost, site toxins, utility conflicts and water budget.
  4. Select documented pair. Consult local nursery, certified scionwood and cultivar-level rootstock data.
  5. Trial conservatively. Follow horticultural guidance and proper seasonal timing. Avoid large public-tree wounds.
  6. Inspect through seasons. Monitor union swelling, dieback, pest signs, breakage, blooms, harvest and maintenance cost.

Grafting timing depends on species and climate. Professional training is recommended before using sharp grafting knives or operating above ground level.

Biological exceptions worth knowing

Some pear cultivars accept quince rootstocks while others require a compatible intermediate piece called an interstock. Cherry, peach and plum all belong to Prunus, but many combinations still fail. Palms, including dates, cannot be grafted by conventional woody cambium methods; they are monocots.

Zero-harm · Anti-inversion. No unauthorized cuts, forced sharing, ecological introductions or unsupported claims. This is a planning archive, not a live tree survey or planting permit.
